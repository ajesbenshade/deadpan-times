ATLANTA — Within minutes of a Massachusetts judge declaring a mistrial in the Lindsay Clancy case, cable news declared the outcome a stunning victory for everyone who had already decided what it meant, and a crushing defeat for everyone else who had also already decided what it meant.

“This changes everything,” said one daytime anchor, before clarifying that it also changes nothing, which is why viewers should stay tuned for a panel that would explain both.

Legal analysts who had summarized six weeks of testimony into ninety-second blocks immediately issued competing six-week analyses. Half concluded that a hung jury proved the system works. The other half concluded that a hung jury proved the system is broken. A third half, discovered mid-segment, concluded that there are only two halves and that math is currently under review.

Court officials scheduled a status conference to determine whether to schedule another status conference about whether to retry the case, a process one clerk described as “the judicial equivalent of hitting snooze on a decision.” Prosecutors said they had not decided. Defense counsel said they had decided prosecutors should decide not to. Viewers at home decided they needed another chyron.

Social media experts warned that the public had already moved on to the next trial, then immediately posted threads about this one. Podcasts announced special episodes titled “What We Know,” “What We Don’t Know,” and “What We Will Pretend to Know for an Hour.”

At press time, networks were teasing a weekend special on what a mistrial means for people who already googled what a mistrial means, while a lower-third promised “breaking analysis” of analysis that had been breaking since lunch.
